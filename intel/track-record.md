## 1. One person, the output of a whole team
When I joined, growth had no AI system — analysis was manual. A few months later,
one person plus Claude Code does the work that used to need an SEO agency, an
analyst, a strategy consultant, and a data analyst. The lever wasn't working
harder. It was building systems that do the work while you sleep.

## 2. The "engine", not the dashboard
I didn't build a dashboard. I built a living system: ~16 automated jobs feeding a
20+ page internal hub that refreshes itself — pipeline, content, competitors, paid
vs. CRM. The difference between a dashboard and an engine is who does the work
after you close the laptop.

## 3. A dashboard that lies is worse than no dashboard
Twice I caught our own hub showing a number that wasn't true — a traffic chart
"crashing" to zero that was really just reporting lag; stale data served as fresh.
The fix wasn't cosmetic. I made the system fail loudly instead of quietly lying.
Trust in your own numbers is the whole game.

## 4. 96% of our organic traffic was branded
I ran the audit expecting to find which blogs to write next. Instead: almost all
our search traffic was people already typing the company name. The channel wasn't
acquiring new demand — it was billing us for people we already had. Lesson:
publishing more content on a domain with no authority doesn't rank. Diagnose
before you produce.

## 5. Trade-show lead capture with zero paper
Built the full stack for a booth: a digital game, a "what does NOT having this
cost you?" simulator, forms — every lead landing in the CRM, attributed down to
which mechanic they used. No clipboards, no manual spreadsheet, no lead lost.
Reusable for the next event in one afternoon.

## 6. Competitive intel that refuses to auto-publish
A data-driven battlecard system: our own side refreshes automatically, but any
claim about a competitor goes to review and email first — never auto-published.
Automate the gathering, keep a human on anything you'd be embarrassed to get wrong.

## 7. I threw out our own battlecard before writing a comparison
We had a "competitor X is legacy, no AI" card. I checked them live before
publishing — false. They had a real, modern product. So I wrote an honest
comparison that conceded their strengths and differentiated only on what's true.
Honest comparisons get cited; dishonest ones get caught.

## 8. Cut an external stack of six vendors down to one
Paid, creative, social, execution — six outside providers, each with a slice and
no single point of coordination. Consolidated to one partner with a clear contact
per function. Fewer hand-offs beat more specialists.

## 9. A thousand sales calls, turned into ad copy
Every recorded sales call, mined for the exact objections and triggers buyers
actually say — then fed into the ad-copy generator. The copy stopped being generic
("Excel isn't built for this") and started quoting the real conversation. Your best
copywriter is the transcript of your own calls.

## 10. A memory system that cleans up after itself
The AI's context kept growing without limit — stale notes, broken links, bloated
state. So I built rotation into it: old logs archive themselves, stale memory gets
flagged, the boot context stays flat. If a system needs manual gardening, it won't
survive six months.
